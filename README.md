**Brief Project Description**: 

In this research project, I investigated the relationship between gradient categorization, linguistic diversity, and L2 speech category learning across three studies using a VAS (visual analog scale) perceptual speech rating task, AX perceptual speech discrimination task, a MCPD (multiple-choice partial dictation) listening assessment task, and a Linguistic Diversity Questionnaire (LDQ). The target L2 speech categories in this project were Japanese vowel length and consonant length.

**Subjects**: applied linguistics, second language acquisition, psycholinguistics, cognitive psychology, cognition and perception, phonetics and phonology

**Keywords**: gradient categorization, linguistic diversity, speech perception, perceptual
learning, L2 speech learning, phonetic training

## Study Abstract

The present study investigated whether exposure to linguistic diversity promotes gradient speech categorization and facilitates second language (L2) speech learning. Speech categorization, or the mapping of continuous acoustic signals onto discrete language units such as phonemes, is a core challenge in adult L2 speech acquisition. Some individuals show gradient patterns of speech categorization, in which the continuous nature of acoustic speech cues is retained in the mental representations of speech categories. Gradient categorization has been found to perceptual flexibility and multiple acoustic cue integration, which may be advantageous for learning new L2 phonemes. Exposure to linguistically diverse environments has been associated with gradient categorization and perceptual flexibility, and specific language experience may support L2 perception when learners have familiarity with phonemic contrasts similar to those in the target language. From these observations, I hypothesized that exposing learners to some form of specific “linguistic diversity” may push them towards gradient perception and the perceptual flexibility needed for efficient L2 speech category learning.

To test this hypothesis, three studies were conducted to explore the relationships among categorization gradiency, linguistic diversity, and L2 learning of the Japanese phonemic length contrasts in vowels (e.g., toru vs. tooru) and consonants (e.g., kata vs. katta). Study 1 assessed whether experiential linguistic diversity, measured with a Linguistic Diversity Questionnaire, predicted gradiency in L1 and L2 categorization, measured with a Visual Analog Scale (VAS) task. Study 2 manipulated linguistic diversity exposure through short-term perceptual discrimination training with naïve L1 monolingual English speakers (training on Japanese alone vs. Japanese and Modern Standard Arabic) and assessed changes in gradiency and L2 discrimination, measured with a perceptual discrimination task. Study 3 extended this training to L2 Japanese learners in a classroom setting and evaluated changes in gradiency, L2 discrimination, and L2 listening accuracy, measured with a Multiple-Choice Partial Dictation (MCPD) listening assessment.

Across all three studies, individual differences in categorization strategies were evident within and across participants, but neither experiential nor manipulated linguistic diversity significantly predicted nor affected gradiency. Additionally, naïve, but not classroom, learners improved in L2 learning outcomes following training, though learning was not modulated by the linguistic diversity condition. Overall, the results of the three studies did not support the hypothesis that exposure to specific linguistic diversity promotes gradient perception and the perceptual flexibility needed for efficient L2 speech category learning. Instead, the findings showcase the diversity of individual differences in categorization gradiency, highlight the phoneme- and context-specific nature of categorization behavior, and provide one of the first few within-participant comparisons of L1 and L2 categorization gradiency. The implications of the findings in the context of extant literature are that gradient categorization may be a stable trait, rather than manipulable by short-term exposure, and that the effects of linguistic diversity on perception and learning are likely to manifest over a longer time period than utilized in this study. This dissertation contributes to the fields of applied psycholinguistics and SLA by offering the first exploration into connecting gradient categorization, linguistic diversity, and L2 speech learning.

# Methodological Information

## Data Collection Dates

**Study 1**: Data were collected on 2025-03-07 and 2025-03-08. Participants were recruited online via Prolific.com.

**Study 2**: Data were collected between the dates of 2025-03-13 and 2025-03-26. Participants were recruited online via Prolific.com.

**Study 3**: Data were collected between the dates of 2025-03-24 and 2025-06-06. Participants were recruited via email distributed by Japanese language professors.

All participants resided and were present in the United States at the time of data collection.

## Project Dependencies

Several Microsoft Office Suite applications were used to plan and draft research materials, record research notes, and track research progress, including **Microsoft Word**, **Microsoft PowerPoint**, and **Microsoft Excel**. 

Stimuli were recorded via the **Praat** desktop application (Version 6.4.12; Boersma & Weenink, 2024[^1]) and **Gorilla.sc** on **Google Chrome**. All speakers were recorded in a sound-attenuated booth with a Shure SM48 microphone fitted with foam windscreen connected via XLR cable to a Focusrite Scarlett 4i4 3rd Gen USB audio interface connected to a 2021 M1 MacBook Pro laptop.

Stimuli were processed with **Praat**.

Data were collected on **Gorilla.sc**.

VAS response curvefitting data were procesed using the **Nonlinear curvefitting for Psycholinguistics package** (Version 48; McMurray, 2017[^2]) on **MATLAB_R2024a**.

Data were cleaned, processed, analyzed, and visualized using **R** (Version 4.4.3; R Core Team, 2025[^3]) with **RStudio** (Versions 2024.12.1-563 and 2025.05.0-496). All R packages used can be found in the following file: [05_Thesis/02_RMarkdown_Writing_Files/bib/packages.bib](05_Thesis/02_RMarkdown_Writing_Files/bib/packages.bib)
